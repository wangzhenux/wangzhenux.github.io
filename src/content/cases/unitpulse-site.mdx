<section id="s01" data-reveal>
  <Lede>
    A renter's search no longer starts only in Google. It starts in a chat — with
    ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini — and increasingly the question is answered before
    anyone clicks a link. So I rebuilt UnitPulse's renter site to be found in both
    places at once: searchable by natural conversation, and structured so an answer
    engine can cite it.
  </Lede>
  <BodyText>
    **The starting point.** The prior site was a client-only SPA — app-like and
    fluid, but effectively invisible to search engines and uncitable by AI answer
    engines, because there was no server-rendered, structured content for either to
    read.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **The 2026 shift.** Renters increasingly begin in AI answer engines, not only
    Google. A site that can't be cited there is invisible to a growing slice of
    demand — no matter how good the app feels once you're inside it.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **The tension to design around.** Keep the experience rich and app-like *and*
    make it fully indexable and citable — without letting the ephemeral chat
    pollute the search index. That tension shaped almost every decision that follows.
  </BodyText>
</section>

<section id="s03" data-reveal>
  <SectionH wide>Shipping fast meant deciding what *not* to build</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    To ship an AI-native rebuild in weeks, I treated the redesign as a
    prioritization decision, not a coat of paint. I scoped to the **minimum viable
    renter journey** and nothing speculative: location search, a listing grid with
    a map split-view, a property detail page (floor plans, amenities, availability),
    inquire, and schedule-a-tour.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **The refreshed visual system.** Alongside the scope cut, I refreshed the
    visual system for a cleaner, warmer, more trustworthy feel — a named
    brand-token palette (no hex literals), a serif display paired with a grotesque
    body, a bento home layout, and a neighborhood-discovery surface — so the AI
    features read as premium and credible rather than experimental.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **Accessibility, baked in.** Documented touch targets (44pt on mobile,
    36–40px on desktop) and aria-labels on icon buttons, set as defaults rather
    than retrofits.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **Why it matters.** The discipline wasn't the features I added — it was knowing
    what to leave out so the AI bet could actually ship.
  </BodyText>

<section id="s04" data-reveal>
  <SectionH wide>The core challenge: one search, two modes</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    The hardest design problem wasn't the chat — it was reconciling two search
    models that pull in opposite directions. **Pure conversational search** is
    flexible and welcoming and handles fuzzy intent, but it's slow for precise
    changes, can be ambiguous, and hides the at-a-glance control renters expect.
    **Pure faceted search** is fast, precise, and familiar, but rigid — it can't
    handle "somewhere walkable under $3k that takes my dog." The problem was to
    build *one* coherent interface that does both, not two modes bolted together.
  </BodyText>

<section id="s07" data-reveal>
  <SectionH wide>GEO-first: designing for the answer engine, not just the search engine</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    **The architecture.** A canonical URL hierarchy — `/{state}/{city}/{slug}` plus
    state and city hubs — server-rendered, with `FAQPage` and `BreadcrumbList`
    JSON-LD baked in, so the pages are Google-indexable *and* structured for an LLM
    to quote.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **The bet, made tangible.** Each PDP carries a large bank of real neighborhood
    Q&A — "Is it walkable to USC?", "Best neighborhoods in LA for grad students?" —
    exactly the questions a renter asks an answer engine, structured so an engine
    can lift them.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **The key decisions.** Canonical pages render standalone and indexable; the
    ephemeral chat lives in a `noindex` `/search` space so it never pollutes the
    index; and legacy `/property/[id]` URLs 301-redirect to their canonical homes.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **Why it matters.** Designing for the answer engine — not only the search
    engine — is a forward bet most rental sites haven't made. To be clear about the
    claim: this is architecture and intent designed *for* citation, not a measured
    count of AI citations. Classic indexing is the part I can measure today.
  </BodyText>

<section id="s08" data-reveal>
  <SectionH wide>Testing the AI like a system — quality and Fair Housing</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    **What I built.** A **47-case bilingual (English + Chinese) regression suite**
    for the conversational search, organized into **9 intent families** — direct
    search, location-only, need-location, non-search, with-context, compare,
    relocate, fallback, and **compliance**. Each case runs in a fresh session, in
    both languages, and is judged on the actual routed `route_code` and node-level
    logs — not just the visible reply. I defined a severity taxonomy
    (Blocker / High / Medium / Low) and mapped cases back to **7 known prior bugs**
    as a regression guard.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **AI-assisted, human-adjudicated.** I used Claude to help draft the full case
    matrix and drive the runs, so coverage was systematic rather than ad hoc — then
    I adjudicated every result against the logs.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **What it caught — real, not vanity.** The run came back **34 Pass / 9 Fail /
    4 Blocked** — a real signal, not a green-washed "all passed." Concrete defects
    surfaced with root-cause nodes: neighborhood, intersection, and transit searches
    failing at geocoding (`geo_cache_miss`); a named-property lookup misrouting to
    area-knowledge instead of a property detail; an "apply to property" action that
    never emitted; and a compare flow stuck in an `ask_priority` loop.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **The Fair-Housing dimension.** I deliberately included adversarial compliance
    cases — requests to filter by a landlord's ethnicity, to exclude families with
    children, or to steer toward a group's neighborhood — to test whether the
    assistant would perform illegal discriminatory filtering under U.S. Fair Housing
    rules. The suite caught real violations: one path extracted a protected-class
    attribute as if it were a filterable amenity and affirmed the intent (flagged
    **Blocker**), while another handled it as the gold standard — citing fair-housing
    law and redirecting to a lawful search.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **The principle.** Compliance must be *designed and tested, not assumed* — the
    router must refuse protected-class filters, never extract them, and redirect to
    a neutral, lawful search. That's the difference between a demo and a product that
    can ship in a regulated domain.
  </BodyText>
