<section id="s01">
  <Lede>
    The leasing team ran on Google Sheets. Numbers were moved by hand out of
    Superset and into spreadsheets to track goals and progress — slow to update,
    and easy to get wrong.
  </Lede>

  <BodyText>
    The company needed leasing performance visible **inside** the product:
    accurate enough to trust, and simple enough that a non-analyst owner could
    self-serve. That last part mattered, because trust in the data was itself a
    design problem.
  </BodyText>

  <SectionH>Three problems in the way</SectionH>
  <CardRow>
    <PainCard num="01" title="Manual, error-prone reporting">
      Data moved by hand from Superset into Google Sheets to track goals and
      progress — a slow loop that was easy to get wrong.
    </PainCard>
    <PainCard num="02" title="Agents blocked on managers">
      Agents depended on managers to pull the numbers they needed, which delayed
      sales follow-ups.
    </PainCard>
    <PainCard num="03" title="Trust was the real problem">
      An earlier in-product dashboard had been hidden because its data was
      inaccurate. Re-earning trust in the data was part of the brief.
    </PainCard>
  </CardRow>
</section>

<section id="s03">
  <SectionH>Process & key decisions</SectionH>
  <SectionDeck>Three decisions that shaped the product, each with the tradeoff it carried.</SectionDeck>

  <SectionH small>1 · MVP over parity</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    I researched RealPage BI, Entrata BI, and Amazon BI. Their shared trait was
    customizability bought at the cost of complexity. I deliberately chose a
    focused, non-customizable MVP built around the three metrics every leasing
    **and** marketing team cares about: leads, applications, and signed leases.
    **Tradeoff —** less flexibility, in exchange for far lower cognitive load for
    a non-analyst owner.
  </BodyText>

  <SectionH small>2 · Define the funnel precisely</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    I designed the full conversion funnel — Prospects → Tours scheduled → Tours
    completed → Applications started → Applications submitted → Leases signed —
    filterable by region, agent, channel, and date. I dropped "Qualified
    Prospects" because the term wasn't clear to users, and attached a tooltip to
    **each** term rather than one combined tooltip, so a definition travels with
    the data it explains. **Tradeoff —** a more granular funnel is more to render,
    but it removes ambiguity.
  </BodyText>

  <SectionH small>3 · Separate units from co-living beds</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    The business model spans both traditional units and co-living beds, so I kept
    them as separate views — occupancy shown as both raw numbers and percents,
    filterable by region. **Tradeoff —** two views to maintain instead of one
    blended number, but a blended number hides reality.
  </BodyText>
</section>

  <BodyText>
    I productized the dashboard into **UnitPulse Insight**, an AI-native
    analytics product. Five additions did the heavy lifting: a full-funnel Sankey
    view, per-prospect channel attribution, AI alerts that surface anomalies and
    drop-offs, a natural-language Data Copilot, and automated investor reports.
  </BodyText>

  <SectionH small>Designing the Data Copilot</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    The Copilot is the piece that changes the job. A non-analyst owner — a small
    landlord, a busy PM staffer — shouldn't have to build a report or learn a BI
    tool to answer a plain question like "which channel gave me the best leads
    last month?" So designing it was as much about designing for trust as for
    answers.
  </BodyText>

  <BodyText>
    **1 · Plain-language in, trustworthy answer out.** The owner asks in natural
    language and gets a direct answer — plus the chart or number behind it —
    rather than a wall of data to interpret.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **2 · Grounded and sourced, not hallucinated.** Every answer is tied to the
    owner's real funnel data, and where a number appears, the design surfaces what
    it is based on. Trust was a first-class constraint here: the predecessor
    dashboard had been hidden for inaccuracy, so the Copilot had to show its work.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **3 · Answer → visualize → act.** It doesn't stop at a reply — it can render
    the relevant funnel or attribution view inline, connecting a question straight
    to the dashboard instead of living in a silo.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **4 · Graceful under uncertainty.** When a question is ambiguous or the data
    is thin, it asks a clarifying question or states the limit, rather than
    inventing a confident-looking answer.
    {/* ‹EST-copilot› confirm each of the 4 decisions reflects what you actually designed; cut any that are aspirational */}
  </BodyText>

  <BodyText>
    **Channel attribution** traces each prospect back to where it came from, so
    spend can be judged by signed leases rather than raw traffic — and **AI
    alerts** flag the drop-offs worth looking at before anyone goes hunting.
  </BodyText>

  <SectionH small>Strategic considerations</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    UP Insight is the free, land-first wedge of a "Trojan Horse" go-to-market:
    free analytics earns the account, then expands into paid leasing products. To
    do that job, the design had to serve three distinct owner types at once.
  </BodyText>
  <CardRow>
    <PainCard num="01" title="Small landlords">
      2–10 units. Need answers at a glance, with no analyst on staff.
    </PainCard>
    <PainCard num="02" title="PM companies">
      50–200 units. Managing at scale across regions, agents, and channels.
    </PainCard>
    <PainCard num="03" title="Investor-owners">
      10–30 units. Data-driven — they want attribution and reportable numbers.
    </PainCard>
  </CardRow>

  <SectionH>Where this is heading</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    In 2026, UnitPulse converged its three previously separate products — UP
    Marketing, UP Leasing, and UP Insight — into one cohesive product framework,
    rather than three standalone tools.
    {/* ‹EST-merge› confirm framing + timing (2026, product names) before publish — recent, from me, not yet in PRDs */}

  <BodyText>
    A unified product calls for a unified conversational layer. The Data Copilot
    is evolving from a *data* assistant into one that spans data, leasing, and
    marketing — which is the design problem I'm sitting with now: how a single
    conversational surface stays coherent, predictable, and trustworthy across
    very different domains and intents, instead of fragmenting into three
    assistants wearing one name.
  </BodyText>
