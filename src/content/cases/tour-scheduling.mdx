<section id="s01">
  <Lede>
    No-shows are expensive. A prospect who books a tour and never arrives burns
    an agent's time and costs the property a chance to lease the unit.
  </Lede>

  <BodyText>
    The day-of reminder flow was the single biggest lever on that loss — it was
    where most no-shows traced back to. And the messages worked against
    themselves: there were too many of them, and they ran too long, which risked
    the worst outcome of all — a prospect opting out of texts entirely.
  </BodyText>

  <BodyText>
    So the brief carried a real tension. **Reminders have to cut no-shows without
    annoying prospects into unsubscribing** — and every message has to stay
    A2P 10DLC and TCPA-compliant. Those weren't concerns to balance later; they
    were the constraint from the first message.
  </BodyText>
</section>

<section id="s02">
  <SectionH>The reminder baseline</SectionH>
  <SectionDeck>What a tour reminder usually looks like — and why it leaks.</SectionDeck>
  <BodyText>
    Most appointment and tour reminders share one shape: a single timed blast a
    day or so out, one-directional with no way to confirm, written in one
    language, and dressed in the platform's own branding rather than the
    property's. Plenty send several messages, which only trains people to mute the
    sender. It's a reminder that *informs* but never asks the prospect to
    *commit* — and that gap is exactly where no-shows live.
  </BodyText>

<section id="s04">
  <SectionH>Design decisions</SectionH>
  <SectionDeck>Six decisions, each one closing a gap in that default.</SectionDeck>

  <BodyText>
    **1 · Interactive confirmation, not a passive reminder.** A short message that
    asks the prospect to *actively confirm* — so the appointment is locked in, not
    just announced. A question is harder to ignore than a notice.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **2 · A language-agnostic confirm.** A single short reply — say, "1" —
    confirms the tour in any language. A "reply YES" prompt quietly excludes
    anyone not reading in English; a number doesn't.
  </BodyText>

  {/* PLACEHOLDER — replace with the real reply-to-confirm SMS screens */}
  <Figure
    src="/cases/tour-scheduling/sms-confirm.svg"
    alt="The reply-to-confirm SMS: a short reminder asks the prospect to reply 1, a one-tap confirm that works in any language"
    caption="The reply-to-confirm interaction — a short reminder, a one-tap reply, a confirmed tour, readable in any language. (Placeholder.)"
    width={1600}
    height={667}
  />

  <BodyText>
    **3 · Right message, right moment.** A booking confirmation on schedule, a
    reminder that carries the confirm ask ahead of the tour, and a clean
    cancellation path. Each message earns its send.
  </BodyText>

  {/* PLACEHOLDER — replace with the real cadence/timeline if desired */}
  <Figure
    src="/cases/tour-scheduling/cadence.svg"
    alt="Message cadence: a booking confirmation on schedule, a 24-hour reminder carrying the confirm ask, and a clean cancellation path"
    caption="The cadence — confirmation, a 24-hour reminder that carries the confirm ask, and a clean way out. (Placeholder.)"
    width={1600}
    height={500}
  />

  <BodyText>
    **4 · Trim to reduce opt-outs.** Fewer, shorter touring messages, with the
    feedback request pulled out of the confirmation — so the essential ask isn't
    buried under things that can wait. Fewer messages are kinder to the prospect
    and safer against opt-outs.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **5 · Brand-aware templates.** Templates adapt to partner and third-party
    properties that lease under their own brand, so a reminder looks like it came
    from the property being toured, not from an unfamiliar platform.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    **6 · Compliance as a design constraint.** A2P 10DLC and TCPA-compliant opt-in
    and message design from the first message, not bolted on — the same
    registration work I'd done on the Twilio side made the rules concrete rather
    than abstract.
  </BodyText>
</section>

<section id="s05">
  <SectionH>The message flow</SectionH>
  <SectionDeck>From a tangle of touring messages to a lean confirm flow.</SectionDeck>
  <BodyText>
    Mapping every touring message in one place made the redundancy obvious.
    Collapsing it to three that each earn their send — a confirmation, a reminder
    that carries the confirm ask, and a cancellation path — is what made the
    system calmer for the prospect and easier to measure.
  </BodyText>

<section id="s07">
  <SectionH>The AI tie-in</SectionH>
  <BodyText>
    The tour flow later became conversational. Scheduling, rescheduling, and
    cancelling a tour are now handled by AI **"Tour Agents,"** and the prospect's
    confirm reply feeds a signal back into the AI leasing system — so a "yes" is
    no longer just a calendar event, it's input the broader system can act on.
  </BodyText>
  <BodyText>
    That connects this lifecycle work to the wider CRM Copilot ecosystem:
    the lightweight confirm interaction designed here became one of the everyday
    signals the leasing AI reads.
  </BodyText>
</section>

<section id="s09">
  <SectionH>Reflection</SectionH>
  <Reflection>
    <ReflectionCard label="On measurement" heading="A first signal, not a verdict">
      A 40% relative drop in month one is a strong start, but one month is a
      signal, not proof. What I'd instrument next is the no-show delta by message
      variant, so the win can be attributed to specific copy rather than to the
      redesign as a whole.
    </ReflectionCard>
    <ReflectionCard label="On what's next" heading="Design it as an experiment">
      The natural next moves are tests, not a one-shot fix: confirm-rate by
      channel (SMS vs email), and reminder timing — does a 24-hour reminder beat
      a same-morning one? Each is a question the messaging system can answer if
      it's built to be measured.
    </ReflectionCard>
  </Reflection>
