Onboarding Twilio's first international tier

How a US-only developer platform learned to feel global, in three deliberate phases.

Role
Senior Product Designer
Lead designer on Regional Onboarding
Team
Cross-functional with Growth, Console, and Phone Number teams
Span
~3.5 months
Outcome
+179% impressions on regional Buy-a-Number page, 3 months after Phase 1

Most US-based developer tools feel global until you launch in Singapore. Twilio Regions had been live for two years; almost nobody outside the US was using it.

Twilio Regions lets customers deploy apps in specific geographic regions. Solves data sovereignty, performance, disaster recovery — the whole basket. But customers either didn't know it existed or built in US1 anyway, even when their users were in Sydney. The product was technically capable. The onboarding wasn't.

Three reasons regional adoption stalled

Before designing anything, I went back through customer support tickets, sales conversations, and product analytics. Three patterns kept showing up.

01

Lack of visibility

A new feature. Many users didn't know the regions existed or what they were good for.

02

Limited use cases

Customers had to evaluate whether their use case justified Regions, but not all features were available outside US1.

03

Technical complexity

Setting it up required re-routing application traffic. Most customers gave up before the first config.

jtbd.png · job-to-be-done framing
The job, in customer language: "As a Twilio customer building for non-US users, I want to know that different regions exist, so I can decide whether building in a non-US region makes sense for my application."

Looking around

How three direct-comparison platforms handle the same onboarding moment.

Before sketching anything, I wanted to know how other developer platforms surface regional or data-residency choices in onboarding. Short version: they mostly don't. Each came close in different ways.

Plivo
Strength Guided onboarding tour walks users through their first build. Calm pace, redact option to anonymize sample data.
Gap The tour is product-only — no region or data-residency framing anywhere in the flow.
Vonage
Strength Filters guides to only the products you've selected. Links into clear data-handling docs.
Gap Region is implied by phone number country, never directly chosen by the user.
Google Cloud
Strength Side-panel tutorial guides region setup explicitly. Makes the choice visible and explained.
Gap Region selection is permanent — no easy way back. High friction for "just exploring" users.

None of the three surfaced data-residency choices in the early onboarding moments.

7 ideas, narrowed to 2

I mapped the existing onboarding flow and brainstormed 7 places to introduce Regions:

  • 01Choose region when naming account
  • 02Choose region in Ahoy questions
  • 03One-pager for region selection between Ahoy and DashboardTested
  • 04Region selection modal first time in Console
  • 05Appcue tour in Console
  • 06Confirm region when buying a phone number
  • 07Confirm region when saving phone number configurationTested

After a RAPID review with Global, Growth, Console, and Phone Number teams, we agreed to test 2 — options 03 and 07. The rest got documented and parked.

User flow with seven possible insertion points — we proceeded with options 3 and 7 after RAPID review.

Surviving design critique

Two critiques landed on similar themes — copy was making benefits feel optional, and modals were carrying too many things at once.

Critique 01 · One-pager region selection

"Default Region" implied billing or language, not data residency

Change: Re-titled the screen "Choose default region," added a region-specific popover explaining product support, and rewrote the copy so the limited-product caveat for non-US1 read as one direct sentence.

design_critique1.png · click to enlarge
Critique 02 · Post-config optimization modal

Informative modal felt overwhelming, doing too many jobs

Change: Removed the default-region setting from the modal (the concept itself was confusing), added a numbered title for relevance, explained why the modal was appearing, and consolidated the two confusing buttons into one per region selection.

A phased approach to "regional by default"

Validating with 10 customers showed they understood the regional concept once it was framed correctly. But the strategic question wasn't "where should this banner go" — it was "how do we get every new customer thinking regional from the start, without breaking the experience for the millions already in US1?"

What I learned

On decision making

RAPID, used early, keeps divergence productive

The seven-ideas-to-two narrowing only worked because we'd already agreed on who recommends, agrees, performs, inputs, and decides. Coming back to that framework when energy fragmented made the path forward obvious.

On strategy

Phased approaches let teams measure as they go

"Get every new customer thinking regional" was never going to ship in one version. Three audiences let the team adjust Phase 2 against routing-pattern data from Phase 1, instead of guessing twelve months out.

Reusable component catalog (applies to every case study, not just Twilio)

Every component below has a CSS class that drops into any case (Tasktop Hub, Tasktop Viz, Rackspace, future Tripalink/UnitPulse). Same tokens, same markup contracts.

  • .nav + .back — top nav (logo, Work, Writing, About) and the "← All work" back link. Identical on every case page.
  • .hero — eyebrow + display-serif title + italic deck + cover image + .meta-strip (Role / Team / Span / Outcome). Cover is a per-case gradient (token: --case-{name}) or real image.
  • .lede — large display-serif paragraph that opens the case. One per case.
  • .body-text — sans body paragraph, fills column. <strong> inside gets the marker-pen highlight automatically.
  • .section-h — section heading with a 28-px accent rule above. Optional .section-deck italic sub-line beneath.
  • .card-row + .pain-card — three-column editorial block. No card chrome; large display-serif numbers + heading + body. For pain points, principles, themes.
  • .competitors + .competitor — competitor row: logo / Strength / Gap. Top-aligned. Drop in 3-4 per case as needed.
  • .research-stats — three-up stat block (Participants / Objectives / Threads) for the research section.
  • .quote-row + .quote-card — three customer-quote cards with monospace label + display-serif heading + italic quote.
  • .pullquote — italic display-serif quote with left accent rail. Use sparingly (1-2 per case).
  • .figure + .figure-caption — image with monospace caption, body-column width. For non-showcase images.
  • .image-showcase — full-bleed band with subtle dotted-grid background, image at body width. Reserved for design artifacts (user flows, mockups, before/afters) — not text. Click any image to open the lightbox.
  • .dual — two-column findings + recommendations side-by-side, hairline-separated rows.
  • .phase + sibling .image-showcase — numbered phase block (text only) with a full-width design mockup beneath. Repeat per phase.
  • .outcome — big metric (160 px display-serif accent number) + supporting text. One per case.
  • .reflection + .reflection-card — two-column "What I learned" with monospace label + display-serif heading + body. Standard close to every case.
  • .critique — text-left + image-right vertical stack. Use 0-3 per case as the design-iteration story warrants.
  • .next-case — footer that links to the next case in the curated order.
  • .toc — sticky right-side navigator. Auto-hides when image-showcase is in view (IntersectionObserver, fades on a 300 ms transition).

Design tokens (single source of truth)

All color, type, and rule values live as CSS custom properties on .preview (--bg-primary, --ink-primary, --accent, --rule, --font-display, --font-body, --font-mono, --case-{name}). Swap palettes (light → dark, accent change, per-case cover gradient) by editing the token block once. No values are hardcoded inside component CSS.

Showcase pattern — what it's for, what it isn't

The full-bleed dotted-grid section is reserved for design artifacts that benefit from breathing room (user flows, phase mockups, future Figma exports). Text content (descriptions, labels, headings) stays in the body column — the showcase is not a "section background," it's specifically a frame for the work itself.

Click-to-enlarge — interaction model

  • Hover state: cursor → zoom-in, image lifts 2 px with a soft drop shadow, ⤢ icon fades in at top-right corner.
  • Click: full-screen overlay with the image centered, dim background.
  • Dismiss: click outside the image, or press Esc.
  • Real implementation: swap the placeholder text for the actual asset; consider <dialog> element + showModal() for a11y, or a small library (PhotoSwipe / Yet Another React Lightbox) if a fuller UX is wanted (next/prev, zoom, captions).

Responsive plan

  • ≥ 1280 px: right-side sticky TOC visible.
  • 1024–1279 px: TOC collapses to thin floating column on right — dots + numbers, names on hover.
  • 768–1023 px (tablet): TOC hidden, top progress bar + section tag takes over.
  • < 768 px (mobile): top progress bar only; tap → drawer with TOC list.

Same navigator system at different breakpoints — sidebar where it fits, progress bar where it doesn't.
